How Small Law Firms Track Client Travel Expenses (Without Losing Billable Hours)

A deposition two states away produces a flight, a hotel, two taxis and a dinner. Each one has to reach the right matter, the right invoice and, eventually, your tax records. Here is how 1–20 lawyer firms handle that, from a spreadsheet to a dedicated travel and expense tool.

The three rules every system has to follow

1. Every expense needs a matter

Client travel is only billable if you can show which matter it belongs to. The most common failure is not a lost receipt; it is a receipt nobody can tie to a client three weeks later. Whatever system you use, the matter or client code should be attached when the trip is booked, not reconstructed at month end.

2. Keep the records the IRS expects

IRS Publication 463 covers travel recordkeeping. As this summary of the rules explains, expenses under $75 generally do not need a receipt, but lodging always does, regardless of amount. For every expense you still need the amount, date, place and business purpose. A note like "Smith v. Jones, deposition" covers the business purpose and the matter at once.

3. Bill travel at actual cost, and say so up front

ABA Formal Opinion 93-379, discussed in this Alabama State Bar opinion, treats a surcharge on disbursements such as travel and lodging as improper unless the lawyer incurred extra cost, and says discounts the lawyer receives should be passed to the client. The same opinion points to Rule 1.5(b): explain how expenses will be billed, preferably in writing, at the start of the engagement. Your engagement letter should say which travel costs are billed and at what rate. Rules vary by state, so check your own bar's guidance.

The spreadsheet: fine at low volume

One row per expense with date, matter number, vendor, amount, business purpose and a link to the receipt in a shared folder. It costs nothing and every lawyer already knows how to use it. The cost is time: someone has to type every line, chase missing receipts and copy billable items onto client invoices. At a few trips a year that is an hour here and there. At two trips a month it becomes a recurring admin afternoon.

Practice management software: good for billing, weak for travel

Most legal practice management tools let you record an expense entry against a matter so it flows onto the client invoice. If your firm already bills this way, keep doing it. The gap is everything before that entry: the trip is booked on a personal card or a travel site, the receipt sits in an inbox, and someone still has to enter it. This works when lawyers are disciplined about logging expenses the same week.

Frequently asked questions

Can a law firm mark up travel expenses billed to a client?

Generally not. ABA Formal Opinion 93-379, as discussed by state bars, treats a surcharge on disbursements as improper unless the lawyer incurred additional cost. Check your state's rules and say in the engagement letter how travel will be billed.

Do I need a receipt for every travel expense?

Not for every one. Under IRS guidance, expenses under $75 generally don't need a receipt, but lodging always does. You still need the amount, date, place and business purpose for each expense. Ask your accountant how this applies to your firm.
